Phone Touch Screen Study

Can one phone turn an ordinary 27-inch monitor into a touch screen, as a step toward a touch-sensitive projected wall? Two placements were tested: phone at the side, and phone low in front like a projector, using its two back lenses for depth.

2 sessions44 targets (32 touch, 12 hover)1,229 analysed stereo frames27" monitor, 2560×1440POCO M4 Pro

Verdict

The phone finds the screen and maps a finger onto it accurately: screen fit 1.5 mm, and 4–14 mm from the target when the hand was tracked. It cannot yet tell a touch from a hover. The two back lenses are 1 cm apart, which gives a depth signal of 1.4 px per 3 cm. The shift wobbles by 3.7 px from frame to frame, so touching and hovering overlap. A touch screen needs a camera that looks across the glass, or two cameras much further apart.

Finding the screen works

Both lenses found 11 of the 15 markers in under a second. Screen mapping error was 1.5 mm, and the two lenses lined up to 1.0 px.

Stereo depth is too weak

Hovering 3 cm off the glass shifted the finger by 1.4 px, but the shift varied by 3.7 px at a single target. Touch vs hover was told apart 66% of the time (50% = guessing).

One camera from the front can't see depth at all

Without stereo, every hover frame (100%) lined up with the screen and would count as a touch. Stereo is necessary; it just isn't strong enough at a 1 cm baseline.

The low angle costs hand tracking

The main lens found the hand in 69% of frames: 80% near the bottom of the screen, 51% near the top. The ultra-wide lens managed only 18%.

Placement A failed for a different reason: the phone ended up facing the screen (flatness 0.74, where a grazing view is under 0.2). From the front, a hovering finger lines up with the screen behind it, so touches never ended: one stayed "down" for 41 s. The app now checks this flatness after calibration and warns when it's too high.

Why stereo depth failed (B)

  • The measurement itself was reliable. The finger patch matched between the lenses with a median correlation of 0.93, and only 1% of frames fell below 0.5. On synthetic images the same method is accurate to 0.04 px.
  • The problem is geometry. The lenses are 1 cm apart and the finger was about 40–60 cm away. A finger 3 cm off the glass shifts by only about 1.4 px, close to the theoretical f·B·d / Z².
  • Real noise is about 100× the synthetic noise. The ultra-wide lens has lower resolution and strong barrel distortion, which a plane mapping can't remove. The finger also looks slightly different to each lens, and the patch behind the tip is partly off the glass. Together these make the 3.7 px spread.
  • The one-point depth calibration recorded a 4.8 px shift at touch and 11 px of noise while hovering, so the "WEAK" warning was correct before the test began.

Hand tracking from a low angle

Mapping is accurate when the hand is tracked: in 4 of the 16 touch tasks the fingertip came within 0–14 mm of the target. The 171 mm median error comes from frames where the tracker lost the hand or picked a wrong point.

Next steps

  1. Repeat placement A with the phone truly looking across the glass, where the app should report flatness under 0.2. This is the quickest route to a working monitor touch screen.
  2. On the projector wall, combine the shadow test with the front view: shadow for touch, front view for position. That fits the presentation project.
  3. If depth from the front is still wanted, use the Mac camera and the phone as a 20–30 cm stereo pair.
  4. Low-angle tracking: crop around the last hand position at full resolution before tracking, and raise the phone slightly so the top of the screen isn't so steep.

Method

  • Screen calibration (B): 15 ArUco markers shown full-screen. Main-lens to screen mapping and ultra-wide to main mapping fitted with RANSAC from the marker corners both lenses saw.
  • Depth: the ultra-wide frame is warped onto the main one so the screen plane lines up. A finger patch just behind the tip is matched with normalised cross-correlation, then refined to sub-pixel. The shift along the direction learned from touch-then-hover gives the depth score.
  • Test: 16 touch targets (10.6 mm radius) and 6 hover targets, random positions, one at a time. Every frame was logged: hand found per lens, screen point, depth score, match quality.
  • Limits: one person, one session per placement. Placement A was set up wrongly, so it is not a fair test of the side method.
