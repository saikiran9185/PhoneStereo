# Phone sonar sees the reach, not the touch

A phone alone cannot tell a hand touching the wall from a hand hovering 1–5 cm away, when the phone sits 2.5–4 m back. This is a physics limit, not a coding problem. The best the POCO M4 Pro can do alone is say "someone just reached into the strip of air near the wall". It cannot say "the hand is on the wall". The sonar's range resolution is about 3–4 cm, and the wall's echo is far stronger than the hand's echo in the same range slot. The phone's other parts (light sensor, Wi-Fi, magnetometer, IR blaster, flashlight) do worse. No published system detects touch on a surface by sound from metres away. Every working tap or touch system puts a sensor on the surface or on the body. So the honest plan has two parts. Keep SonarMap as the "someone is reaching" sensor. Add a cheap helper on the wall for the moment of contact. The best helper is **4–6 kitchen-foil zones behind a chart-paper poster, read by an ESP32 or an MPR121 board (MPR121 ₹73–115)**. It is the only cheap sensor that also catches a silent, still palm. A **₹20–26 piezo disc** on the same ESP32 adds sharp tap timing. The Mac joins the two signals with a small state machine. Do a short on-device test first (19 kHz loopback, `track.getSettings()`), because the phone's audio limits are not published.

## Sonar at 3 m can't split a 1–5 cm gap

Sonar measures distance by timing echoes. Two things closer together than the range resolution fall into one slot and look like one echo. The rule is ΔR = c / 2B, where B is the chirp's bandwidth. SonarMap's audible chirp (2–8 kHz, B = 6 kHz) gives about **2.9 cm**. The quiet band most phones can use (18–22 kHz, B = 4 kHz) gives about **4.3 cm**. These are my calculations. Real phones do worse. A MobiCom 2023 study measured that a factory phone separates two targets reliably only when they are **7 cm apart** ([PowerPhone](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12765216/)). Phones record at 48 kHz even when the chip could do more. Asking an app for a higher rate does not help, because Android just interpolates and "the true hardware rate stays at 48 kHz" ([PowerPhone](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12765216/)). PowerPhone reached 1 cm resolution only by rooting phones, rewriting the audio driver and running at 192 kHz. One Xiaomi phone (Redmi Note 9 Pro) worked that way, but stock flagships did not ([PowerPhone](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12765216/)). Even 1 cm would only just touch the 1–5 cm hover gap, and nobody showed it against a wall at 3–4 m.

A second problem is echo strength. The wall is a big flat mirror for sound. The hand is a small scatterer. My rough physics estimate puts the hand echo **30–40 dB below the wall echo** at 3.5 m, inside the same 3–4 cm slot. That is 1,000 to 10,000 times weaker in power. Chirp sidelobes are usually −13 dB, or −30 to −40 dB with windowing, so the hand hides under the wall's own echo. Subtracting an "empty wall" recording removes the wall. What is left only says "something changed at the wall's distance". A hand at 0 cm and at 2 cm both land in that slot, so both look the same.

Phase tracking does not save it. Systems like LLAP reach **3.5 mm** accuracy and under 15 ms latency on stock phones ([LLAP](https://samsonsjarkal.github.io/KeSun/files/mobicom16_ultrasound.pdf)). FingerIO reaches about 8 mm ([FingerIO](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~gshyam/Papers/fingerio.pdf)). But these work within about 0.25–1 m of the phone. Phase measures *movement*, not *where*, and at 20 kHz it repeats every 0.86 cm. So a hover of 0.86 cm looks the same as contact. The best published result at room scale from a phone is CovertBand. It tracked whole-body movements up to **6 m with 8–18 cm error** ([UW News](https://www.washington.edu/news/2017/08/16/computer-scientists-use-music-to-covertly-track-body-movements-activity)). That is ten times too coarse. Room-scale hand gestures at **4 m with over 90%** accuracy needed a smart-speaker mic array, not a phone ([SpeakerGesture](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3560905.3568528)).

Distance hurts too. Stock phones sense fine motion to about **1 m** and human presence to about **2 m** ([PowerPhone](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12765216/)). Moving a hand from 0.5 m to 3.5 m away costs about 34 dB of echo strength (my estimate, from the 1/R⁴ rule). The phone speaker is close to omnidirectional, and SonarMap uses one mic. So every object at 3.5 m lands in the same slot: other parts of the wall, the board frame, a student in the front row. Range alone gives a shell around the phone, not a spot on the wall. The only touch-vs-hover result from phone sound used a phone placed on or next to the surface ([Tsuruta, HCII 2016](https://www.iplab.cs.tsukuba.ac.jp/paper/international/tsuruta_hcii2016.pdf)). Even with sensors on the body, hover is the hard case: in ActiTouch, **75.8% of false positives** were hover counted as touch ([ActiTouch](https://yangzhang.dev/research/ActiTouch/ActiTouch.pdf)).

## Listening and the other sensors do no better

A soft touch makes almost no sound. Occupied classrooms run about **48–55 dBA on average** ([Korean classroom study](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO202432043355275.pdf)), and up to **77 dBA** in noisy UK activities ([Shield et al.](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/1496173)). At 3–4 m the phone also sits deep in the room's echo field. So a fingertip touch is effectively silent from the projector. A deliberate knock or palm slap can be heard. But it looks like a desk knock, a dropped pen or a clap. The phone's two mics are about 15 cm apart (not verified for this model). That gives at most ±21 samples of delay at 48 kHz, enough for a rough left/right angle on a sharp knock. It cannot place a point on the wall. Chrome also gives true stereo on some phones and a copied mono channel on others ([addpipe](https://blog.addpipe.com/recording-true-stereo-audio-using-getusermedia/)). Every tap system that works well couples to the surface. Scratch Input put a stethoscope mic on the surface and worked on painted walls at about **90%** for six gestures ([Scratch Input](https://www.chrisharrison.net/index.php/Research/ScratchInput)). Toffee needed the device resting on the table, with **4.3° bearing error** ([Toffee](https://www.robertxiao.ca/research/toffee/)).

The non-sound sensors fail for clear reasons. The light sensor is one wide pixel. A hand blocks about 0.5–1% of the image light, while slide changes swing it by tens of percent (my estimate). With the phone 20–30 cm from the lens, the hand's shadow sits only about 2–3 mm from the hand, so touch and hover look identical. Chrome also hides the light sensor behind a flag ([W3C log](https://lists.w3.org/Archives/Public/public-device-apis-log/2021Sep/0033.html)). The IR blaster can only send; Android has no receive call ([ConsumerIrManager](https://developer.android.com/reference/android/hardware/ConsumerIrManager)). Wi-Fi sensing needs channel data (CSI) that phones with MediaTek chips do not expose ([nexmon_csi](https://github.com/seemoo-lab/nexmon_csi)). Wi-Fi RTT has about **2 m** error at the 90th percentile ([AOSP](https://source.android.com/docs/core/connect/wifi-rtt)). Android also limits Wi-Fi scans to **4 per 2 minutes** ([Android docs](https://developer.android.com/develop/connectivity/wifi/wifi-scan)). The phone has no barometer ([Xiaomi specs](https://www.mi.com/sg/product/poco-m4-pro/specs)). Its proximity sensor works at a few cm, and may be Elliptic Labs' ultrasound "virtual" sensor ([GSMArena](https://m.gsmarena.com/xiaomi_poco_m4_pro-11388.php)).

One method does tell touch from hover without a camera: the body as an antenna for mains hum. It reached **98% touch vs no-touch** and **4.1 cm RMS error** on hand-to-wall distance ([Cohn et al., CHI 2011](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2011/05/chi2011_rfgestures_cohn.pdf)). But it needs a pad touching the person's skin, a 10 MΩ input and sampling at 400 kS/s. A wire in the phone's mic jack, 3 m away, sees only a tiny, noisy ripple. That makes it a body-worn helper, not a phone method.

## The cheapest reliable fix is foil behind a poster

The helper's only job is to say "contact, now". Sonar can keep the job of "who is reaching". Here is how the cheap options compare. Prices come from November 2026 Indian listings where cited. The ESP32 board price was not confirmed, so I estimate ₹400–800 for a generic board.

| Helper | Cost | Touch vs 1–5 cm hover | Still palm? | Latency to the Mac | Notes |

| **A. Foil zones + ESP32 touch pins or MPR121** | MPR121 ₹73–115 ([Robocraze](https://robocraze.com/products/mpr121-12-channel-capacitive-touch-sensor-controller-module-i2c-interface), [Probots](https://probots.co.in/mpr121-capacitive-touch-sensor-controller-breakout-board-12-key.html)); ESP32 ~₹400–800 (estimate); foil | Good for a palm; weaker for a fingertip vs a close palm | **Yes** | Wi-Fi, a few to tens of ms, with spikes | The only cheap sensor for hold and release |

| C. Old phone taped to the wall | ₹0 if you have one | Good for taps | No | Wi-Fi | Chrome motion sensors cap near 60 Hz ([Chromium](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src.git/+show/HEAD/services/device/generic_sensor/README.md)), so use its mic |
| D. Wrist strap + mains-hum front end | Few hundred ₹ + ESP32 | Measured 98% / 4.1 cm ([CHI 2011](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2011/05/chi2011_rfgestures_cohn.pdf)) | Yes | ~100–200 ms | Presenter only; needs per-room training |
| E. ToF sensor along the wall | VL53L0X ₹289 ([Probots](https://probots.co.in/laser-range-finder-distance-sensor-time-of-flight-vl53l0x.html)) | Poor: the 27° cone is ~48 cm wide at 1 m ([ST](https://www.makerguides.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/VL53L1X-Datasheet.pdf)) | — | Wi-Fi | Use only as an X-position aid |

Foil wins because capacitance rises steeply as the hand closes the gap, and jumps again at contact. That gives a real "touching" signal even for a silent, still hand. Wall++ proved painted-wall electrodes can sense touch and hover, at about **$20/m²** in conductive paint ([FIGLAB Wall++](https://www.figlab.com/research/2018/wall)). Foil behind a removable poster is the classroom-friendly version. A simple RC sensor with a 10 MΩ resistor sensed a finger up to about **10 cm** away ([MIT 6.810](https://hcie.csail.mit.edu/classes/2021-fall-6810/lab2-sensing-touch-button.html)). So hover shows up as a smaller rise, and touch as a bigger one. Standard practice is two thresholds, one for hover and a higher one for touch ([US10481723](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10481723)). Bigger pads raise the baseline and false-trigger from nearby hands ([Zbotic ESP32 guide](https://zbotic.in/esp32-touch-sensor-capacitive-input-for-smart-switches/)). So use 20–30 cm pads, not one huge sheet. Humid rooms will drift the baseline. The fix is to re-zero whenever the sonar says nobody is near the wall.

Do **not** plug the piezo into the sonar phone's 3.5 mm jack. This is my inference, but it matters. Android switches both input *and* output to the headset when a TRRS plug goes in, so the sonar would lose its speaker and mic. A raw piezo on a ~2 kΩ mic input also loses most low-frequency energy (high-pass near 4 kHz with a 20 nF disc, by my estimate), and the ideal input impedance is about 1 MΩ ([Logos Foundation](https://logosfoundation.org/g_texts/Orfeus/preamps/piezo_disks.html)). Put the piezo on an ESP32 analog pin instead, with a 1 MΩ resistor across it and two clamp diodes. One ESP32 then serves both foil zones and the piezo.

The fusion idea is already patented by Intel. A far sensor sees a fingertip near the surface and moving toward it. Only then does the system listen for a vibration, and touch is declared when both agree ([US9971457](https://patents.google.com/patent/US9971457)). Here sonar plays the far sensor, and foil or piezo plays the contact sensor. The states are below.

| State | Enter when | Leave when |
|---|---|---|
| AWAY | Start; or no sonar motion in the wall shell for 1.5 s | Sonar "reach score" passes its threshold in 2 of 3 pings → NEAR |
| NEAR (armed) | Sonar sees motion in the wall gate or 10–60 cm in front of it | Foil zone passes the touch threshold, or a piezo onset arrives → TOUCH. No helper event within 2 s → AWAY |
| TOUCH | Helper event while NEAR, or up to 1 s after NEAR ended (covers the 300–400 ms sonar delay) | Next update → HOLD |
| HOLD | Foil zone still above the touch threshold | Foil falls below the hover threshold → RELEASE. Piezo-only zones: auto-release after 150 ms |
| RELEASE | Helper drops | Back to NEAR if sonar still sees motion, else AWAY |

Three rules make this robust. First, any helper event while AWAY is dropped. That kills door slams and desk knocks, which is Intel's arming logic. Second, a foil event while AWAY but above a high "palm flat" threshold is still logged, so you can see later whether the sonar missed it. Third, the touch position comes from the foil zone ID, because the sonar has no angle. The browser's ~275 ms delay does not hurt ranging, because SonarMap re-measures the speaker-to-mic delay from the direct path every ping. It only makes NEAR arrive late. People take about half a second to reach a wall, so a 1 s look-back window absorbs it. Use the ESP32's own event time as the touch time, so the user sees the foil's speed, not the sonar's delay.

## Build plan inside SonarMap

The plan keeps SonarMap's shape: the phone page does sonar, `server.mjs` on the Mac relays and logs, and `view.html` shows results. Three things in today's code limit it. `correlate()` keeps only the magnitude of the matched filter, so background subtraction misses phase changes. The chirp is only 4 ms long, which wastes echo energy at 3 m. And `flush()` posts every 400 ms, which is too slow for a state machine. The steps below fix these in order. Step 0 measures the unknowns before any new feature.

| Step | Change | Pass test |
|---|---|---|
| 0. On-device audio test | New `test.html`. Log `track.getSettings()` (echoCancellation, noiseSuppression, autoGainControl, sampleRate, channelCount), plus `ctx.sampleRate`, `ctx.baseLatency` and `ctx.outputLatency`. Play 2 kHz and 19 kHz tones at the same gain into the phone's own mic, and post both FFT peak levels and the noise floor. Repeat with `channelCount: 2` and check that the two channels differ. Optional via adb: `dumpsys sensorservice` and `pm list features` | Processing really off. 19 kHz within ~15 dB of 2 kHz with good SNR, the level Android "strongly recommends" for near-ultrasound ([Android CDD 7.8](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/compatibility/cdd/+/refs/tags/android-platform-12.1.0_r10/7_hardware-compatibility/7_8_audio.md)). If it fails, stay on 2–8 kHz |
| 1. Wall lock | New "Wall" mode next to Map and Theremin. Aim the phone's bottom edge at the wall, with a paper cone on the speaker to narrow the beam. "Lock wall" records 30 empty pings, finds the wall peak past the blind zone with the existing sub-bin fit, and stores it | Wall distance repeats within ±5 mm over 100 pings at 3 m |
| 2. Complex background | Make `correlate()` return I and Q per lag, not just the envelope. Store the empty-scene I/Q. Score = magnitude of (current − empty) in a gate of the wall ±2 bins, plus a "body" gate 10–60 cm in front of the wall. Try a longer chirp (8–12 ms) for more echo energy | Reach score clearly separates "empty" from "standing still at 50 cm" from "hand moving at the wall" across 20 trials each |
| 3. Measure the limit honestly | Log the score for: standing still, hover 5 cm, hover 1 cm, touch (20 trials each, both bands) | Expected: touch ≈ hover. This confirms the sonar's role as "armer" only |
| 4. Faster posts | Add `reach`, `body` and the ping time (from `ctx.getOutputTimestamp()`) to a small `/api/sonar` post every ~100 ms. Add `/api/time` for a ping-based clock offset | Mac log shows sonar state at least 8 times per second |
| 5. Helper hardware | ESP32 with 4–6 foil zones (20–30 cm) behind chart paper on short wires, plus a piezo on an ADC pin (1 MΩ + clamp diodes). Firmware keeps a slow baseline per zone with two thresholds (hover, touch) and runs a piezo onset detector. It POSTs `{zone, kind, raw, t}` to a plain-HTTP listener on the Mac (for example port 8080), since TLS on the ESP32 is extra work | Palm flat: ≥95% detected. 1–5 cm hover: below the touch threshold most of the time. Firm taps: ≥90% |
| 6. Fusion | New `fusion.mjs` in the server running the state table above. Broadcast `touch`, `hold` and `release` over the existing SSE stream. Show the state and the active zone in `view.html` | With 3 students moving nearby: ≤1 false touch per minute; door and desk knocks rejected |
| 7. Auto-recalibrate | Re-zero the foil baselines and the sonar empty-scene whenever the state is AWAY for over 10 s | No drift-caused false touches over a 40-minute class |

If the step 0 test shows Chrome is still filtering audio, the fallback is a small native Android app using Oboe with the `Unprocessed` input preset. That path is dependable but still runs at 48 kHz ([PowerPhone](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12765216/)). Its main gain is latency (about 20–50 ms instead of 300–400 ms, my estimate), not resolution.

## Conclusion

The question "touch or hover?" is about the last centimetre. A distant sensor can only answer it if it resolves that centimetre, and nothing in a stock phone does at 3–4 m. So the real design choice is not which algorithm, but where the contact sensor lives. Once foil sits on the wall, the phone's sonar changes job. It stops trying to find the touch and starts doing the two things the foil can't: ignoring knocks when nobody is reaching, and telling the system when the wall is empty so it can re-zero. That makes the sonar useful, even though its accuracy is too coarse to detect touch on its own.

Three unknowns remain, and only the phone can answer them. Does Chrome on this POCO really turn processing off and pass 19 kHz? How much does a paper cone narrow the beam in a real classroom? And does foil behind chart paper separate a fingertip from a hovering palm on these walls? Nobody has published these numbers. Run step 0 and the foil bench test first, before you build anything else.
